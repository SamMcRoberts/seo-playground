The down-payment treadmill

How long until a working family can actually buy?

Saving for a larger down payment lowers financing costs. But waiting while home prices rise can erase that advantage. This model shows both sides of the tradeoff instead of pretending the target price stands still.

Your assumptions

Change anything that does not look like your household.

The defaults use current national source data plus two explicit forecasting assumptions: 3% annual home-price growth and 3.5% yield on down-payment savings.

5% vs 10% vs 20%

The cheapest mortgage is not necessarily the cheapest path to ownership.

“Lifetime cash cost” means purchase down payment + 30 years of principal and interest + modeled PMI. Property tax, insurance, maintenance, selling costs, and opportunity cost are excluded because they are not financing differences between the three down-payment choices.

Time to target

How long the family waits.

Bars begin at zero. The target home price is allowed to grow during the saving period; the savings balance earns the selected yield.

Method

What the model is doing.

No frozen home price. No hidden assumptions. Every input that materially changes the answer is exposed above.

Saving period

Each month, the household adds income × savings rate ÷ 12. Existing savings accrue at the selected yield. The home target appreciates at the selected annual rate until savings equal 5%, 10%, or 20% of that future price.

Mortgage

The model uses a fully amortizing 30-year fixed mortgage at the selected rate. It reports monthly principal-and-interest and total interest over 360 payments.

PMI

For sub-20% down scenarios, PMI is modeled as the selected annual percentage of the original loan until modeled loan-to-value reaches 80%, using the same home-price-growth assumption.

Important: BEA’s personal saving rate is measured against disposable income, not gross household income. Applying 3.0% to gross income makes the national default somewhat optimistic. The savings-rate field is deliberately editable.
